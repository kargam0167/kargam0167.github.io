A Human Computer Interaction course usually starts with studying the interface: a screen, a button, a task, a user trying to get something done. This autumn I taught a master's course at Rīga Stradiņš University, "Anthropology of the Digital: Information, Memory, and Culture", and my main argument is that this starting point is too narrow. Before anyone clicks anything, a culture has already decided what counts as a "user", what counts as "relevant", and what is worth measuring. I think that is the part of HCI that gets studied least, and it is where anthropology helps most.

The course opens with a simple question: what is lost when a social practice becomes data? We look at clicks, GPS traces, and engagement as thin descriptions. They record that something happened, not what it meant to the person doing it. From there the course moves down into the machinery. The "cloud" turns out to be cables, minerals, and energy. Interfaces turn out to be part of how people remember and think together. And the rules of a system, its licences, protocols and defaults, decide who is allowed to change it. Students probed generative AI systems (GPT, Claude, Gemini) through persona role-play, treating their responses as culturally saturated performances: a method that reveals the model's textual performance of culture. When we position the conversational agent as an active co-constructor of meaning, we understand that its ability to shift human meanings through social framing may be exploited for persuasion. Teaching students to read these outputs as cultural performances, rather than as neutral information, may be one of the most important skills an HCI course can offer.

> Generative systems are now among the most prolific performers of culture we have — repeating its templates, smoothing its rough edges, and feeding the refined versions back to us.

Further, we dive into who the system imagines on the other side of the screen. We read Bowker and Star, and Espeland and Sauder, on how values become categories, rankings, and permissions. We read Wajcman and Yuk Hui on why expertise and "universal" design are never neutral, and Lupton on self-tracking, where a measurable proxy such as screen time quietly stands in for a whole person. We then looked at intimacy through Madianou and Miller's idea of "polymedia": choosing WhatsApp over a call, or a voice note over a text, is already a statement about a relationship. Every one of these is an interaction-design question, asked from the side of lived experience. In parallel, we treated recommender and ranking systems as culture: built by people with ideas about taste, shaped by company goals, and interpreted by users through their own "folk theories". 

> HCI tells us how people use a system. Anthropology asks what the system assumes about them, and what they make of it.

This is also a part of my own research on digital representation of taste. The habit of mixing product or service design with close anthropological reading is what I would like students to take out of the course. If you design, evaluate, or study information retrieval, I think three habits are worth borrowing:

- Ask what a metric actually leaves out before you optimise it.
- Look for the infrastructure and the people behind a "seamless" experience.
- Treat users' folk theories as data about your system.
